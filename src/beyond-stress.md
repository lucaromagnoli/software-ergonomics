# Beyond stress

<p class="tagline">Stress is where the case is clearest — not where the discipline ends.</p>

<p class="lede">It is tempting to read software ergonomics as a response to crisis: travel disruptions, urgent transfers, emergencies, safety-critical activities. Those situations dominate this site's examples because they expose the consequences of poor ergonomics in a clear and memorable way. But they are the demonstration, not the definition.</p>

## A chair is not ergonomic only when you are injured

Physical ergonomics does not wait for harm. A chair is not considered ergonomic because it helps the injured or the exhausted; it is considered ergonomic because it reduces effort and discomfort during ordinary, everyday use — and because years of ordinary use without it produce the injury.

The same principle applies to software. Software ergonomics seeks to reduce unnecessary cognitive effort in *all* interactions — whether the user is relaxed, focused, stressed, tired, experienced, or opening the product for the first time. In everyday use that looks unglamorous:

- Clear terminology and language.
- Sensible defaults.
- Predictable behavior.
- Consistent navigation.
- Progressive disclosure of complexity.
- Minimal interruptions.
- Reduced decision fatigue.
- Efficient workflows.
- Respect for user attention.

None of these is a crisis feature. Each one lowers the baseline cost of using the software — and the baseline is what most of a user's life with a product is made of. Stress-aware design is one important application of the philosophy, not the philosophy itself.

## A misconception about engagement

Modern products are optimized for engagement, feature discovery, retention, conversion, and monetization. Somewhere along the way, this hardened into an assumption: more prompts, more notifications, more reminders, more interaction — better outcomes.

Software ergonomics challenges that assumption, and so do users. Many of the most beloved software products are praised not for demanding attention but for staying out of the way. Listen to how people describe the software they trust:

- "It just works."
- "It stays out of my way."
- "It lets me focus."
- "It feels natural."
- "It respects my time."

Every one of these is an expression of good software ergonomics. Nobody has ever praised a product by saying it interrupts them at exactly the right frequency.

## Ergonomics and product success

It would also be a mistake to file software ergonomics solely under accessibility, wellbeing, or ethics. Those framings are true, but incomplete — and they make it too easy to deprioritize.

Better ergonomics tends to make better products. Reducing unnecessary cognitive burden can produce:

- Greater user satisfaction.
- Higher retention.
- Increased trust.
- Faster task completion.
- Fewer mistakes.
- Stronger long-term engagement — the kind that comes from preference, not capture.

Ergonomics and business objectives are not necessarily in conflict. A product that respects human cognitive limits may ultimately outcompete one that continuously fights for attention — because users, given a choice, walk toward the tool that costs them less to use.

## A broader discipline

Stress-aware design is one pillar. The discipline, as it develops, plausibly encompasses:

- **Cognitive load management** — treating attention and working memory as budgeted resources in every design decision.
- **Stress-aware design** — degrading gracefully as the user's capacity degrades; the subject of much of this site.
- **Attention management** — interruption policy, notification design, and who gets to spend the user's focus.
- **Security ergonomics** — security flows that work with human limits instead of depending on them; [the password is the cautionary tale](index.html#passwords-the-case-study-we-already-ran).
- **Workflow efficiency** — minimizing the steps, decisions, and re-orientations between intent and outcome.
- **Progressive complexity** — interfaces that start simple and reveal depth as competence grows, rather than presenting everything at once.
- **Safety-critical interaction** — software behavior when attention is already required elsewhere; [the driving example](scope.html#a-worked-example-driving).

Together these serve a single objective — the one in the masthead: designing software that works with human cognition, not against it.

The ultimate goal of software ergonomics is not merely to make software less stressful. It is to make software more compatible with the way human beings think, decide, learn, focus, and interact with the world.
